Putting yourself in the shoes of others is only the first level of empathy

22 September 2026

It is where most people stop, and they miss all the free intel sitting just past it.

I ask the same question in workshops, in schools, in companies and in public offices, all around the world. What is empathy? Almost every single time the answer that comes back is putting yourself in the shoes of others. That is the dictionary version, it is a genuine skill, and it improves every part of a life, so I am not here to tell you it is wrong.

But that phrase lets you assume you already know what somebody is thinking, either because something like it happened to you once, or because you pictured it in your head and the picture felt convincing enough. Both of those are guesses about you. Acting on them is how you end up certain you are helping and find out later that you made it worse.

A child is crying in the playground. From across the yard it looks like a fall, so you walk over and tell them they are fine, up you get, it is only a scraped knee. Then you get close enough to smell what has actually happened. They have soiled themselves, they are mortified, they have no idea what to do next, and you have just told them their real problem is nothing.

It is an odd example to lead with and I am using it because it happened to me. It also makes the point better than a tidier one would. I have taken plenty of falls and scraped plenty of knees, and none of that told me anything about what that child was feeling.

The textbooks split empathy into cognitive, emotional and compassionate. Those are accurate and there is not much you can do with them on a Tuesday afternoon, so here are three levels you can practise instead.

They live in the third of the five spheres, the Direct Stakeholder. The first two spheres are about you, and the third is where the work turns outward and asks one question: to what extent am I serving the people I want to impact? Everything below is how you answer it.

Each row is the same two people, you and them, drawn before and after.

Level one puts you in their shoes, and it is still your perspective, so it does not mean you are seeing everything. Level two squeezes as much of your perspective as you can into their lens by choosing to believe what they tell you, even where you do not agree with it, and that is the discomfort you can see in the tight fit. Level three is the expansion, for both of you, and it comes from deep curiosity: asking about what you know, about what is not being said, and about what your intuition is pointing at.

Level one, putting yourself in the shoes of others

At this level you can say what somebody is feeling and thinking, and you can say who your stakeholders are and what they do. You are doing it from a safe, analytical distance, with the demographics, the survey results and the behaviour charts. An empathy map gets you a long way here: what they see, what they hear, who they hear it from, what hurts and what they want.

Do not skip past this one. Plenty of people cannot get even this far, and it is the level where training works fastest.

Then comes the part that catches everybody, including me. You are still not your customer. I am the father of twin boys, born the same day, in the same classroom and in most of the same sports, and they live and experience life completely differently. If the gap is that wide between those two, it is wider between you and the people you are trying to serve.

But there are things you can do to narrow it. Bodystorming puts you physically into their situation before you design for them, which is how Patricia Moore dressed as a senior citizen across 116 cities to feel the challenges of ageing first hand, and ended up giving us OXO Good Grips. People journey mapping tracks their experience over time instead of in a single moment.

Both are worth doing, and both are built on your interpretation. You are still guessing, just more intelligently.

Level two, choosing to believe them as they experienced it

The biggest thing missing from the everyday definition is what comes after you have put yourself in their shoes. You have to believe them. Brené Brown says it better than I can:

In order to empathize with someone’s experience you must be willing to believe them as they see it and not how you imagine their experience to be.

That means holding your judgement, which is the thing almost everybody finds hardest. You will know you are on track because you will feel resistance, and the closest thing I can compare it to is swallowing your pride. Let it go and you will notice your body relax, and that is the moment you start actually hearing them.

This is where most leaders come unstuck, and it is almost never because they do not care. It is their expertise. A customer tells you they are struggling to get onboarded, you answer that it was not that hard for you, and in one sentence you have told them their experience is a reporting error.

The drawing calls this a tight, uncomfortable fit, which is honest. You are further into their world than is comfortable, and you have given up the part of you that was standing outside checking their story against your own.

Level three, getting so curious you hear what they are not saying

Two things are going on at this level. You are listening at the top of your range, on top of everything you have mapped, observed and asked. And, counterintuitively, you are paying close attention to your own physical, mental and emotional state, because that is where the signal usually turns up first.

That is why I call it intuitive curiosity. Curiosity is the engine that keeps you digging. The intuition is everything you have ever absorbed, surfacing when something in front of you matches a pattern you have met before, and it tends to arrive in your body before it arrives as a thought.

This is what people are describing when they talk about a gut feeling, and it turns up in different ways. Sometimes it is the classic pull in the stomach. Sometimes it is a racing heart, and sometimes it is tears.

The first feeling you get is rarely the answer. It is usually the topsoil, so you take a layer off, ask whether that is really what you are sensing, and dig again until one layer gives you the click of recognition. Most people stop two layers up and act on the surface.

Henry Ford is widely credited with saying that if he had asked his customers what they wanted, they would have said faster horses. People often cannot tell you what they need because they have not dug for it themselves. Level three is where you help them get past the faster horses, and yourself at the same time.

Look at what happens to both circles in that last panel. Your curiosity grows them and it grows you, which is not what anybody expects from something that sounds like giving your attention away.

What to do with this

Some people tell me they have the opposite problem, that they have too much empathy and cannot carry what they end up taking on from other people. That is real, and it is not a reason to stay at level one. It means your empathy needs something steering it, which is what compassion is for, and the four tenets are the practice I point people to.

If you have been putting yourself in other people's shoes for years and it has not taken you as far as you expected, that is because it was only ever the first of three. The next time you catch yourself disagreeing with how somebody felt about something, hold the judgement, wait for the resistance, and let it go.

That is level two. It costs you something, and it changes what the person in front of you is willing to say next.
