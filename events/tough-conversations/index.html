Letting Someone Go

Six years ago I had to let someone go. I’d done it before and thought I was good at it. I was broke, exhausted, and scared about money, and I carried all of that into the room with me.

I still think about it. I don’t think they do. But I do.

That’s the part nobody warns you about: the conversation doesn’t end when you leave the room. It lives on in whoever you had it with. Or, if you’re the type that cares how you show up, in you too.

Many of us never practise this. And those who are taught something are usually taught one method: rip the band-aid off. Say it, say what happens next, get in, get out. It’s not wrong: done well, it’s humane. Dragging it out is its own cruelty.

But the band-aid works best when you’re delivering someone else’s decision. When it’s yours, your call, your company, your values in the room, clean and fast isn’t enough. You have to work out what to say, and how, from what you actually believe.

So that’s what this is. A bit of practice at both: the band-aid, for when that’s the job, and the harder conversation, built from your own values, for when it’s on you.

110 minutes on saying the hard thing, so the person across from you walks out still respected, and still in your corner.

What’s actually at stake isn’t the news. It’s whether you lose an ally.

People can take hard news. What they can’t take, what they carry for years, is walking out thinking that company never really cared about me. And then telling that story to everyone they meet.

Any company can look like it cares when things are good.

Here’s the test. The real proof is how a leader shows up in the one moment they’re forced to let someone go. If you’re the kind of company that cares about its people, that’s exactly where you have to show it, or admit you never were.

Get it right, and the person leaves still an ally. Get it wrong, and you lose them twice: the role, and the relationship.

The whole session turns on one question: did they leave feeling respected? Only one person can answer it: the one who received it.

You don’t just hear it. You do it.

Same shape as the pitching and sensing drills: a short piece of theory, then everyone practises. You’re put in a pair. One delivers, one receives, then you swap.

You both score it. The receiver’s score is the one that counts, because they’re the only person in the room who can answer the question this whole session turns on.

Bring a real one you’re carrying, or take one of these. They’re here so nobody has to put their own on the table in a room of strangers:

  • Letting someone go, when it wasn’t your decision to make.
  • Letting someone go who’s also dealing with something serious at home.

Two ways to do it, and you’ll practise both

The band-aid. Clear, fast, humane. For when you’re carrying out a decision that’s already made, and the kindest thing is not to drag it out. There’s a craft to it: said badly, fast becomes cold.

The one built from your values. For when the decision is yours, and how you do it says who you are. Slower, harder, and the one most leaders have never actually practised.

Five steps hold the second one together, and not one of them is a compliment sandwich. The sandwich keeps you comfortable while the other person leaves unsure what was actually said. This starts somewhere better: from what you both already agree on.

  • Start from shared purpose and common ground.
  • Name what’s gone wrong, and what it’s costing.
  • State the decision plainly.
  • Offer a way forward: what you recommend, what support exists, what happens next.
  • Thank them for their time and their contribution.

Half of it happens before anyone speaks

Settle yourself first, or you make it about you. Physical, mental, emotional preparation, the same way the Sensing Drill handles it. You settle, you come back to why you’re doing this at all, and only then do you open your mouth. Skip it and you say the thing you’ll be apologising for in a year. This is Sphere 2, the Conscious Self, and it’s the half of the conversation that happens before a word is said.

Then: their body is telling you something, and so is yours. Reading the other person is the obvious half. Noticing what your own shoulders, hands and jaw are doing to them is the half people miss. We take both apart, then you practise with someone watching.

What you leave with

A structure you can use on Monday, and an honest read on how you did: your own scores, the scores from people watching, and the score from the person who actually received it. Where those three disagree is the part worth your attention.
