Letting Someone Go

Six years ago I had to let someone go. I’d done it before and thought I knew how to do it properly. I was broke, exhausted, and scared about money, and I carried all of that into the room with me.

I still think about it. I don’t think they do. But I do.

That is the part nobody warns you about. The conversation does not end when you leave the room. It lives on in whoever you had it with. Or, if you are the type that cares how you show up, in you too.

110 minutes on saying the hard thing, so the person across from you walks out still respected, and still in your corner.

Before the drill itself

Most of us never practise this

Those who are taught something are usually taught one method: rip the band-aid off.

  1. Say it.
  2. Say what happens next.
  3. Get in.
  4. Get out.

It isn’t wrong, and done well it is humane, because dragging it out is its own cruelty.

The band-aid works best when you are delivering someone else’s decision.

When it is yours, your call, your company, your values in the room, sometimes clean and fast doesn’t feel right. You have to work out what to say, and how, from what you actually believe.

So that is what this is. A bit of practice at both: the band-aid, for when that is the job, and the harder conversation, built from your own values, for when it is on you.

What is at stake

It isn’t the news. It is whether you lose an ally.

People can take hard news. What they can’t take, what they carry for years, is walking out thinking that company never really cared about me. And then telling that story to everyone they meet.

Any company can look like it cares when things are good.

Here is the test. The real proof is how a leader shows up in the one moment they are forced to let someone go. If you are the kind of company that cares about its people, that is exactly where you show it. If you are not, that is where you admit you never really cared about them.

Get it right and the person leaves still an ally. Get it wrong and you lose them twice: the role, and the relationship.

The whole session turns on one question: did they leave feeling respected? The only person who can answer that is the person who received it.

What happens on the night

A short piece of theory, then everyone practises

Same shape as the pitching and sensing drills.

  1. You are put in a pair.
  2. One of you delivers the feedback.
  3. The other receives it.
  4. You swap.
  5. You both score each other’s delivery.

The receiver’s score is the one that counts, because they are the only person in the room who can answer the question this whole session turns on.

Bring a real one you are carrying, or take one of these. They are here so nobody has to put their own on the table in a room of strangers:

  • Letting someone go, your call.
  • Letting someone go, when it wasn’t your decision to make.
  • Letting someone go who is also dealing with something serious at home.
What you are taught

Two ways to do it, and you will practise both

The band-aid. Clear, fast, humane. For when you are carrying out a decision that is already made, and the kindest thing is not to drag it out. There is a craft to it: said badly, fast becomes cold.

The one built from your values. For when the decision is yours, and how you do it says who you are. Slower, harder, and the one most leaders have never actually practised.

Here are the five steps that hold the second one together, and not one of them is a compliment sandwich. The sandwich keeps you comfortable while the other person leaves unsure what was actually said. This starts from what you both already agree on.

  1. Start from shared purpose and common ground.
  2. Name what has gone wrong, and what it is costing.
  3. State the decision plainly.
  4. Offer a way forward: what you recommend, what support exists, what happens next.
  5. Thank them for their time and their contribution.
What you leave with

A structure you can use on Monday, and an honest read on how you did

Your own scores, the scores from the people watching, and the score from the person who actually received it. Where those three disagree is the part worth your attention.
