# Telling the Time: the twelve trivia facts, checked

Checked 24 November 2026, against the twelve facts as they stand in
`tools-for-parents/telling-the-time/index.html`.

The brief asked for all twelve checked with sources before they go public. The
facts in the page are G's own text, carried over verbatim and not rewritten
here. Three items do not survive the check and one is advice rather than fact.
Proposed replacement wording is given for each of those, in English only, for
G to approve before the Polish, Mandarin, Indonesian and French versions are
written to match.

Verdicts used below:

- **holds**, the claim is supported by a primary or near-primary source
- **soft**, a generalisation that is not false but cannot be cited to anything
- **wrong**, the claim is contradicted by the source
- **not a fact**, the sentence gives advice rather than stating something checkable

## The three that need G

### Hour 9, New York Stock Exchange: wrong

The page says the opening bell rings at 9 o'clock. The NYSE opening bell rings
at **9:30 a.m. Eastern**, and has for decades. The core trading session runs
9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. ET and the opening auction is at 9:30 a.m. ET on NYSE's
own trading-hours page.

This one cannot be hedged, because the whole point of the card is the hour it is
keyed to, and the real hour is half past nine. A 9 o'clock fact has to be a
different fact.

Source: <https://www.nyse.com/trade/trading-information> and
<https://www.nyse.com/markets/hours-calendars>

Proposed replacement, still gentle and still about nine in the morning:

> In many countries the working day and the school day both start at about
> 9 o'clock in the morning, which is why the roads are busiest just before it.

That is a soft claim rather than a hard one. If G would rather keep a hard,
citable fact on this hour, an alternative is Big Ben: the Great Bell of
Westminster first chimed on 11 July 1859, and its hour bell has struck every
hour since, nine o'clock included. That is citable but it is not specific to
nine, so the first option reads better on the card.

### Hour 2, the autumn clock change: wrong about which hour repeats

The page says the clocks go back at 2 o'clock in the morning and that
2 o'clock therefore happens twice. Both halves are true somewhere, but not in
the same place:

- In most of the **United States and Canada**, clocks go back **at 2:00 a.m.**,
  to 1:00 a.m. So the hour that happens twice is **1 o'clock**, not 2.
- In the **European Union**, including Poland, summer time ends at 01:00 UTC,
  which is 03:00 local going back to 02:00. So the hour that happens twice
  **is** 2 o'clock, but the change happens at 3, not at 2.

So the sentence as written is accurate in neither jurisdiction. The extra hour
of sleep is correct everywhere.

This is the item G corrected by hand on 24 November, replacing an earlier false
"watch posters" fact, so it is flagged rather than rewritten.

Source, United States: <https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/know-daylight-saving-time-ends-sunday-rcna178497>
Source, European Union: Directive 2000/84/EC, which sets the change at 01:00
Coordinated Universal Time, <https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2000/84/oj>

Proposed replacement, which is true in Poland and in the rest of the EU and
keeps the extra hour:

> In Poland and across Europe, the clocks go back one hour on an autumn night,
> so 2 o'clock in the morning comes round twice and everybody gets an extra
> hour of sleep.

If the card should hold for a reader in the United States too, drop the place
and the hour: "On one autumn night each year the clocks go back an hour, and
everybody gets an extra hour of sleep."

### Hour 10, children's bedtime: not a fact, and the guidance disagrees

The page says 10 o'clock at night is a good bedtime for children. The
guidance does not support that for the age this app is built for. The American
Academy of Sleep Medicine, endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics,
recommends **9 to 12 hours** of sleep per 24 hours for children aged 6 to 12. A
10 p.m. bedtime against a 7 a.m. school start is 9 hours, the very bottom of
the range, and most seven-year-olds need more.

It is also the only one of the twelve that tells the reader what to do rather
than telling them something. A card a child reads at bedtime arguing for a
later bedtime is working against the parent holding the tablet.

Source: <https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/sleep/Pages/healthy-sleep-habits-how-many-hours-does-your-child-need.aspx>
and the AASM consensus statement, <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27250809/>

Proposed replacement, a fact about ten o'clock rather than advice:

> By 10 o'clock at night most children are already asleep, and the body does
> its growing in the first few hours of sleep rather than the last.

## The ones that hold

### Hour 1, the Greenwich time ball

Holds, and the detail is right. Installed in 1833 by John Pond, the sixth
Astronomer Royal, on the roof of Flamsteed House at the Royal Observatory, so
that ships on the Thames could set their chronometers. It still drops at 1 p.m.
daily: it rises halfway at 12.55, to the top at 12.58, and falls at 1 p.m.
exactly. The time is taken from the start of the descent.

One small thing the card does not say and does not need to: it is at 1 p.m.
rather than noon because the astronomers were busy observing the sun crossing
the meridian at noon.

Source: <https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/greenwich-time-ball-one-time-all>

### Hour 4, British afternoon tea

Holds as written, because of the hedge it already carries. "Said to have
started in the 1840s" is the right strength: Anna Russell, Duchess of Bedford,
is widely credited with it around 1840, and the story that she was hungry in
the late afternoon is the traditional account rather than a documented one.
Some sources put her tea at 5 p.m. rather than 4, and some hold that afternoon
tea as a meal predates her. Four o'clock as the traditional hour is
nonetheless the standard account.

Do not strengthen the wording. "Said to have" is doing necessary work.

Source: <https://www.historic-uk.com/CultureUK/Afternoon-Tea> and
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Russell,_Duchess_of_Bedford>

### Hour 5, five o'clock

Holds. Five o'clock as the conventional end of the working day is well
established, and "it's five o'clock somewhere" is a real idiom, carried into
wide use by the Alan Jackson and Jimmy Buffett song of 2003. The card explains
the idiom rather than asserting a historical event, which is the right shape
for it.

### Hour 11, the Armistice

Holds, and it is the best sourced of the twelve. The armistice was signed in a
railway carriage in the Forest of Compiegne in the early hours of 11 November
1918 and took effect at 11 a.m., which is where "the eleventh hour of the
eleventh day of the eleventh month" comes from. Annual remembrance at that hour
is still observed.

The framing in the card is remembrance rather than battle, which was G's
instruction and is correct for a seven-year-old. Keep it.

One caveat worth knowing and not worth putting on the card: fighting did not
stop everywhere at 11 a.m., and other fronts had closed earlier, Turkey on
30 October and Austria-Hungary on 3 November 1918.

Source: <https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/how-did-the-armistice-end-the-first-world-war>
and <https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/milestones-to-peace-armistice-compiegne>

### Hour 12, noon and midnight

Holds for the purpose. The one quibble is that the sun is at its highest at
solar noon, which drifts from clock noon by up to a quarter of an hour or more
depending on where you are in your time zone and the time of year. For a
seven-year-old learning that twelve comes round twice a day, the card is right
and the quibble does not belong on it.

## The soft four

Hours 3, 6, 7 and 8 are generalisations: school bells at about three, sunrise
and roosters at about six, breakfast at seven, school starting at about eight.
None of them is false. None of them can be cited to a source, because there is
no single school day, sunrise or breakfast time to cite.

They are fine on a card that says "many schools" and "in many places", which
all four already do. The recommendation is to leave them and not to attach a
false precision to them. The note is here so that the next person checking does
not read a missing source as an unchecked fact.

## Still outstanding

**Indonesian and French need a native speaker.** The brief asks for this and it
has not been done. Twelve facts each in Bahasa Indonesia and in French, carried
over from the working version, machine-written and never read by a native
speaker. They are public-facing now. Nothing in this pass checked them and
nobody should assume it did.

The Polish and Mandarin strings are a different case: those were corrected by G
against a native reading and the corrections are recorded in the page source.
They are carried over verbatim.
